# Assessment Design

## Build the role scorecard

Start from the full JD and recruiter-provided context, but do not assume the stated candidate profile is complete or correct. Build 3–6 important, distinguishable dimensions by triangulating three sources:

1. **Stated profile:** what the organization says it needs in the JD, recruitment copy or interviewer brief.
2. **Task-derived profile:** capabilities implied by recurring work, difficult decisions, stakeholders, handoffs, constraints and common failure modes.
3. **Uncertain hypotheses:** plausible but unverified qualities that should be tested or confirmed with the hiring owner rather than silently scored as fact.

Run a short scenario scan before finalizing the scorecard:

- What does this person repeatedly do in an ordinary week?
- What becomes costly if done badly, late or inconsistently?
- Which stakeholders have conflicting needs?
- What happens under peak workload, ambiguity, disagreement, failure or handoff?
- Which unglamorous tasks determine reliability and retention?
- What might the recruitment copy overemphasize because it is attractive to candidates?
- What might it omit because the team has normalized it or does not know it is important?

When stated and task-derived criteria conflict, show the conflict and its likely consequence. Ask the hiring owner to resolve decision-changing conflicts when possible; otherwise keep the criterion provisional and avoid using it as a hidden veto.

Translate the combined result into the scorecard:

| Dimension | Why it matters in the work | Basis | Confidence | Weight | Positive evidence | Risk evidence | Best assessment method |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|

Weights express relative importance, not false precision. Mandatory eligibility conditions sit outside the weighted score and are checked separately.

Define each dimension as behavior in the target role. Replace a broad label such as “leadership” with the relevant behavior: taking responsibility under ambiguity, making a decision with incomplete information, aligning others or resolving disagreement. Do not copy one employer's competency words to every role.

Basis values: explicit, task-derived, prior evidence, or uncertain hypothesis. Confidence reflects confidence in the criterion itself, not the candidate. Distinguish these from eligibility/logistics such as location, schedule, authorization, start date or credentials.

## Choose the assessment method

Use a written test when the work is best observed through a bounded artifact: analysis, coding, writing, research synthesis, prioritization, design or data work. Use an interview for motivation, ownership, judgment, collaboration, consistency and live reasoning. Use both only when each adds distinct evidence.

Avoid unpaid speculative work that reproduces substantial real business deliverables. Keep scope proportional to the role and state time limits, permitted tools, expected format and evaluation criteria.

## Generate two question layers

### Part A: role-core assessment

For each high-priority scorecard dimension, choose the evidence source that most closely resembles the work:

- written test for a bounded work product or asynchronous reasoning;
- interview for ownership, motivation, collaboration, judgment and live clarification;
- both only when each answers a different decision question.

Create a shared core for the hiring round. Candidates may receive adaptive follow-ups, but the underlying dimensions and evidence standard stay comparable.

### Part B: candidate-specific verification

Audit the resume, application form, portfolio, submitted task and earlier answers. Create a short verification map:

| Material claim or gap | Why it matters | What is uncertain | Best verification question | Related scorecard dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Prioritize central claims, unclear ownership, AI/team contribution, contradictions, unusually strong evidence, missing prerequisites and experiences that may transfer to the role. Personalized questions verify evidence; they must not introduce irrelevant personal judgments or reduce time needed for the role-core assessment.

Label every planned question `role-core` or `candidate-specific`. When one question serves both purposes, say which shared standard it tests and which personal claim it verifies.

## Market and workplace realism check

When the user wants realistic contemporary hiring, do a focused external scan before designing the assessment:

1. recent comparable JDs from real employers;
2. formal structured-interview or assessment guidance;
3. current domain workflows, tools, regulations and constraints;
4. credible recent interview reports as secondary evidence of question phrasing and process.

Record source date and distinguish recurring role requirements from one employer's convention. If the market has materially changed, prefer current evidence over a stale question bank.

Make each major assessment item pass this test:

- **Job relevance:** maps to a real task, decision or failure mode.
- **Representative constraints:** includes realistic time, information, stakeholder, budget, quality or handoff limits.
- **Observable evidence:** lets the candidate show reasoning, action, artifact, verification or reflection.
- **Fair comparability:** uses a shared standard without demanding identical backgrounds.
- **Tool realism:** states whether AI, internet, code, templates or collaboration are allowed and how their use is evaluated.
- **Proportionality:** does not demand free production work beyond what is necessary to assess the role.

Useful real-work formats include prioritizing an overloaded task list, reviewing a flawed draft, responding to a last-minute change, writing a stakeholder update, planning a handoff, interpreting user feedback, debugging an error, defending a tradeoff and conducting a short postmortem. Choose only formats representative of the target work.

## Design a written test

Include the scenario, necessary context, deliverables, answer format, timebox, permitted AI/internet/code/reference use, allowed assumptions, evaluation dimensions and submission checklist.

Test representative decisions, not lookup trivia unless recall is job-critical. If AI use is permitted, assess framing, decomposition, verification, judgment and disclosure of AI contribution—not the ability to hide AI use.

Present the full test in one batch. Do not provide model answers before submission. Evaluate the artifact, reasoning, factual reliability, tradeoffs, instruction-following and contribution boundary.

## Design the private interview map

| Priority | Layer | Dimension | Level 1 question | Level 2 evidence probes | Level 3 independent re-verification | Stop condition | Time budget |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Cover the highest-weight dimensions first. Include at least one credibility check on a central resume claim and one two-way-fit or logistics segment when relevant. Do not require a fixed question count; fit the map to available time.

Level 2 probes stay with the candidate's first answer and request missing evidence: personal part, concrete process, result, feeling, rejected alternatives and verification. Level 3 changes to an independent experience, scenario or angle to test the same capability when the first story remains inconclusive. Changed constraints and counterfactuals are optional variants; do not use hostility or trick questions.

## Calibrate before starting

Record duration, interviewer roles, must-cover dimensions, question ownership, evidence that would change the decision, prohibited/irrelevant topics, note fields and the decision rule.

For panels, one person controls flow. Observers capture assigned positive or risk evidence without interrupting. A recorder checks missing dimensions before close. Compare differing interviewer impressions against the recorded evidence; the differences themselves may reveal first-impression bias or evidence one interviewer missed.
