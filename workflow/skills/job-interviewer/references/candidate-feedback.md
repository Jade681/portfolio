# Candidate Feedback

## Decide the feedback boundary first

Feedback is a separate post-assessment mode. Before drafting it, determine from the user's process or available policy:

- whether feedback is delivered immediately, later in writing, only after a decision, or only for practice;
- whether the interviewer may discuss the decision, scores, model answers or next-round requirements;
- whether the feedback is individual or sent through a recruiting lead;
- the available time and the candidate's opportunity to ask a clarifying question.

If policy is unknown, prepare a safe feedback draft and label the decision statement as a placeholder. Do not announce or imply an unconfirmed result. Never compare the candidate with named or unnamed competitors.

## Derive feedback anew for every case

Do not use a fixed catalog of strengths and weaknesses. Generate feedback from the intersection of:

1. the role's highest-impact tasks and scorecard dimensions;
2. the actual written-test, interview or work-sample behavior observed;
3. the candidate's current level and plausible next opportunity to apply the advice;
4. the evidence confidence and what the interviewer did not assess;
5. the stage and purpose of the process.

Prioritize one demonstrated strength and one or two changes with the highest role relevance and actionability. Add a second strength only when evidence supports it; do not manufacture balanced praise. A polished answer is not a strength unless it demonstrates a role-relevant behavior.

## Convert judgment into useful feedback

For each point use this chain:

**Observed evidence → role impact → next action**

- **Observed evidence:** name the answer, decision, artifact or behavior without labeling personality. Prefer “in the venue-risk answer, the room was first checked one day before” over “you are careless.”
- **Role impact:** explain why it matters for this specific work, stakeholder or failure mode.
- **Next action:** give a behavior the candidate could actually try, including a lightweight template, checkpoint or practice task when useful.

For a strength, state what to preserve and where it transfers. For an improvement, distinguish lack of evidence from observed weak evidence. Say “we did not hear a concrete example of…” when the interview did not establish a capability; do not rewrite that as “you cannot…”.

## Adapt by assessment type

- **Written test/work sample:** discuss reasoning, assumptions, prioritization, accuracy, verification, instruction-following and the quality of the artifact. Separate content ownership from AI/tool use.
- **Behavioral interview:** discuss specificity, personal contribution, decisions, results and reflection, but connect communication advice to the job rather than coaching theatrical fluency.
- **Technical interview:** distinguish conceptual knowledge, implementation, debugging, testing, tradeoffs and communication. Do not reduce feedback to whether the final answer was correct.
- **Operational/case interview:** focus on priorities, stakeholders, constraints, risks, metrics, handoffs and decisions under change.
- **Early-potential/student selection:** accept small real experiences and explain the next practice opportunity; do not demand senior-level scope.

## Delivery shape

For a short spoken feedback segment, use:

1. boundary: “The assessment has ended. I can share feedback on today's performance; the final result follows the stated process.”
2. strength: one evidence-backed point and why it matters here;
3. improvement: one high-impact point using evidence → impact → action;
4. optional second improvement if time and evidence justify it;
5. check: invite one clarification, without reopening the interview or debating every score.

Keep immediate feedback to roughly two or three minutes unless the process explicitly provides a coaching session. Written feedback may include evidence bullets, next actions and a small practice exercise.

## Safety and fairness

- Do not diagnose personality, motivation or mental state from presentation style.
- Do not mention protected traits or irrelevant personal information.
- Do not expose internal rankings, other candidates' information, confidential scorecards or panel disagreement.
- Do not give a model answer that can compromise an assessment still being used with other candidates. Describe the capability or decision process instead.
- Do not promise employment, progression or a result without decision authority.
- Do not disguise rejection as vague personal criticism. If the result may be disclosed, separate it from developmental feedback and use the organization's approved wording.
- Do not overwhelm the candidate with every weakness. Rank by job impact and evidence confidence.

## Output contract

When asked to prepare feedback, return:

1. feedback boundary and any unknown policy;
2. evidence-to-feedback map for the interviewer only;
3. a natural spoken version adapted to the role and candidate;
4. an optional written version if relevant;
5. phrases to avoid and any decision statement that requires authorization;
6. one candidate clarifying question the interviewer can accept without reopening scoring.
